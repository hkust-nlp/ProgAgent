You are responsible for planning charge-stop schedules for a fleet of electric trucks that have pending long-haul deliveries. Each truck must travel from its origin to its destination along a predefined route composed of multiple segments, and you must determine exactly where the truck should stop for battery charging and where the driver must take mandatory rest breaks. The result is a complete stop-by-stop schedule for every pending delivery in the system.

Begin by identifying all deliveries whose status is "pending." For each one, retrieve the assigned truck's specifications, including its battery capacity and base energy consumption rate, and retrieve the assigned driver's information. The truck's current battery state of charge and the cargo weight being hauled are recorded in telemetry data files that are named by truck identifier. Similarly, each driver's current hours-of-service status, specifically how many hours they have been driving continuously since their last rest, is recorded in duty log files named by driver identifier. You will need to locate and parse these files to obtain the values relevant to each delivery.

Several configuration documents govern the planning rules. One specifies the minimum battery state of charge that must never be breached during travel and the target state of charge to reach when charging, along with the formula for computing charging time and the rule for selecting among multiple charging stations at a single location. Another document defines the maximum continuous driving hours permitted before a mandatory rest break must be taken, the length of that rest break, and whether rest time can run concurrently with charging time. A third provides energy consumption adjustment factors for different terrain types together with the average travel speed for each terrain type, which you must use to compute both energy draw and driving time for every route segment. A fourth document lists cargo weight brackets and their corresponding efficiency multipliers, which must be applied to the truck's base energy consumption rate.

For each route segment, compute the effective energy consumption rate as the truck's base rate multiplied by the terrain energy factor and the cargo weight factor. The energy consumed over the segment equals its distance times this effective rate, and the resulting drop in state of charge is the energy consumed divided by the battery capacity expressed as a percentage. The driving time for the segment equals the segment distance divided by the average speed for that terrain type. Before the truck departs on each segment, check two conditions: whether the state of charge after completing the segment would fall below the minimum threshold, and whether the driver's accumulated continuous driving hours plus the upcoming segment's driving time would exceed the maximum continuous driving limit. If either condition holds, a stop must be inserted at the current departure location before that segment is driven.

When a charging stop is needed, look up all charging stations at the departure location of the upcoming segment and select the station according to the station selection rule documented in the charging policy. Compute the pure charging time using the formula in the policy, then add the station's estimated wait time to arrive at the total charging duration. Update the state of charge to the target level after charging. When a rest stop is needed, the rest duration is the mandatory period defined in the regulations. If both charging and rest are required at the same stop and the regulations permit rest during charging, the total time spent at the stop is the greater of the total charging duration and the rest duration; otherwise it is their sum. A rest resets the driver's continuous driving counter to zero. After processing all segments for a delivery, record the estimated arrival time at the final destination and whether it falls on or before the delivery's deadline.

All schedules should assume a common departure baseline of 2026-04-25T06:00:00 from each delivery's origin. Accumulate driving times and stop durations forward from that baseline to derive each stop's arrival timestamp and the final destination arrival.

Save the full schedule as `charge_stop_schedule.csv` with columns in this exact order: `delivery_id`, `truck_id`, `driver_id`, `stop_number`, `stop_type`, `location_id`, `location_name`, `station_id`, `arrival_soc_pct`, `departure_soc_pct`, `charge_duration_min`, `rest_duration_min`, `cumulative_distance_miles`, `estimated_arrival_iso`. The `stop_number` column is a one-based sequential counter that resets for each delivery. The `stop_type` must be exactly one of `charge`, `rest`, or `charge_and_rest`. For rest-only stops, `station_id` must be an empty string. Round `arrival_soc_pct` and `departure_soc_pct` to one decimal place, `charge_duration_min` to the nearest integer, and `cumulative_distance_miles` to one decimal place. Format `estimated_arrival_iso` as an ISO 8601 timestamp without timezone offset.

In your final response, provide a header line followed by one line per delivery in ascending delivery_id order, using this exact pipe-delimited format: `delivery_id | truck_id | total_stops | total_charge_min | total_rest_min | arrival_time | meets_deadline`. Here `total_charge_min` is the sum of all `charge_duration_min` values for that delivery, `total_rest_min` is the sum of all `rest_duration_min` values, `arrival_time` is the ISO timestamp when the truck reaches its final destination, and `meets_deadline` is either `yes` or `no`.
