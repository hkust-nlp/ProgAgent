You are a logistics risk analyst responsible for identifying shipments that may miss their contractual delivery deadlines. Your organization manages a global shipping portfolio with vessels currently in transit across major trade lanes. Each shipment has a binding contract delivery date, and late arrivals carry financial penalties that scale with the shipper's contract tier. Your job is to produce a comprehensive, prioritized alert list of every at-risk shipment along with the recommended first-priority contingency action for each.

Begin by reviewing the configuration files available to you. A JSON file describing risk thresholds defines four severity tiers, each with a minimum delay in days that a shipment must exceed to qualify for that tier. Shipments whose estimated delay falls below the lowest tier threshold are considered on-track and must be excluded from the final alert list. A YAML file covering international commercial terms maps each incoterm to a risk bearer, either the seller or the buyer, which determines which party's contingency playbook applies. A JSON contingency playbook then maps each combination of risk level and risk bearer to an ordered list of recommended actions; the first action in each list is the most critical and should be reported as the recommended action.

Next, retrieve all shipments currently in transit. For each such shipment, obtain the vessel's current AIS navigation data, which includes the vessel's estimated time of arrival at its destination port. This vessel ETA serves as the baseline for the expected arrival calculation.

On top of the vessel ETA you must layer several additional time components. First, query the destination port's congestion data to obtain the average berth waiting time in days. Second, query the customs clearance statistics for the specific destination port and commodity code combination to obtain the average clearance time in hours, then convert that to days. Before using the raw customs clearance time, check whether a customs modifier applies to that port-commodity pair by consulting the CSV files that list clearance multipliers along with alert levels; if a multiplier exists, apply it to the base customs hours. Additionally, check whether the shipment has been granted a priority upgrade through a contract amendment recorded in the YAML amendment files; if so, reduce the adjusted customs hours by the stated percentage before converting to days.

Separately, consult the JSON route deviation reports to determine whether the shipment's vessel has accumulated any additional delay hours due to weather events, mechanical issues, route deviations, or other disruptions. Sum all delay hours for that vessel and convert to days. Also review the markdown port advisory bulletins for the destination port; if an advisory carries a non-zero additional wait days figure, add the largest such figure for that port to the total.

The expected arrival date is computed as the vessel ETA plus the sum of port congestion wait days, adjusted customs clearance days, route deviation delay days, and port advisory extra wait days. Before comparing this to the contractual deadline, check the YAML contract amendment files for any deadline extensions granted to the shipment; if an extension exists, add those days to the original contract delivery date to obtain the effective deadline. The delay in days is then the difference between the expected arrival date and the effective deadline, counted in whole days.

Once you have computed the delay for every in-transit shipment, discard any shipment whose delay is less than the minimum threshold defined in the risk thresholds configuration. For all remaining shipments, classify each into the appropriate risk tier by checking the thresholds from highest severity downward and assigning the first tier whose minimum delay is met. Determine the risk bearer from the incoterms reference using the shipment's incoterm, then look up the recommended contingency action as the first entry in the playbook for that risk level and risk bearer combination. Also retrieve the shipper's penalty rate per day and multiply it by the delay days to produce the estimated financial penalty.

Finally, sort the alert list first by risk level in descending severity order, with the most critical tier appearing first, and within the same risk level sort by delay days in descending order so the most delayed shipment appears first. Save the result as `alerts.csv` containing exactly these columns in this order: `shipment_id`, `risk_level`, `delay_days`, `vessel_id`, `vessel_name`, `destination_port`, `contract_delivery_date`, `expected_arrival_date`, `incoterm`, `risk_bearer`, `recommended_action`, `estimated_penalty_usd`. Every date should be formatted as YYYY-MM-DD and delay days should be an integer. The estimated penalty should be a numeric value rounded to two decimal places.

In your final response, state the total number of in-transit shipments analyzed, the total number of at-risk shipments identified, a breakdown of how many shipments fall into each risk tier, and briefly describe the top three most critical shipments including their shipment identifiers, risk levels, delay days, vessel names, destination ports, and recommended actions.
