Your department has been tasked with a comprehensive review of all customs import declarations currently on file. Each declaration corresponds to a supplier invoice for goods entering the country, and concerns have been raised that some declared customs values may be systematically understated, leading to significant underpayment of duties. Your job is to identify every invoice whose adjusted landed cost falls more than ten percent below the corresponding market benchmark and compile a detailed discrepancy report for the audit team.

To carry out this analysis you will need to draw on several sources of information. An invoice database is available that can be queried for declaration details such as the declared unit value in the supplier's local currency, the product identifier, supplier identifier, quantity, and invoice date. Shipment records linked to each invoice provide the port of entry and the trade term under which the goods were shipped. Separately, a collection of reference files contains exchange rate tables mapping each currency to its US dollar equivalent, freight and insurance cost ledgers recording the transportation and coverage charges for every invoice, supplementary cost records listing packaging and handling fees per invoice, port-specific surcharge schedules, and a valuation policy document that lays out the precise rules for computing the adjusted CIF value and classifying risk. An incoterm reference file is also available describing which cost components must be added for each trade term.

The valuation methodology works as follows. First, convert each invoice's declared unit value into US dollars using the applicable exchange rate, then multiply by the quantity to obtain the total declared value in dollars. Next, determine whether freight and insurance costs should be added based on the shipment's trade term, following the adjustment rules in the valuation policy and the incoterm reference. A port-specific surcharge, computed as a percentage of the freight cost using the rate from the port surcharge schedule, is always added regardless of trade term. Packaging and handling charges from the supplementary cost records are likewise always included. Divide the resulting total customs value by the invoice quantity to arrive at an adjusted CIF value per unit. The discrepancy percentage is then the amount by which the adjusted CIF per unit falls below the benchmark for the product's harmonized system code, expressed as a fraction of that benchmark and multiplied by one hundred. Only entries where this percentage strictly exceeds ten should be flagged and assigned a risk tier as defined in the valuation policy.

Once all invoices have been evaluated, save the flagged entries to a file called `discrepancy_report.csv`. The columns must appear in exactly this order: `invoice_id`, `supplier_name`, `product_name`, `hs_code`, `declared_cif_usd`, `benchmark_usd`, `discrepancy_pct`, `risk_level`. Values in the `declared_cif_usd` column represent the adjusted CIF per unit rounded to two decimal places, `benchmark_usd` is the market benchmark unit price rounded to two decimal places, and `discrepancy_pct` is the discrepancy percentage rounded to two decimal places. Rows must be ordered by `discrepancy_pct` from highest to lowest, with ties broken by `invoice_id` in ascending alphabetical order.

In your final response, state the total number of flagged entries, the total estimated underpayment amount in US dollars rounded to two decimal places, and a count of entries in each risk tier. The total underpayment for each flagged entry equals the difference between the benchmark unit price and the adjusted CIF per unit, multiplied by the invoice quantity, and the overall total is the sum of these amounts across all flagged entries.
