As a dispatch coordinator for Metro Field Services, today's schedule requires building optimized routes for every available technician in the fleet. The goal is to assign all pending service jobs to technicians while minimizing total travel time across the fleet, respecting every constraint, and producing a definitive stop-by-stop route for each technician.

Each technician has a defined skill level, a shift window, and drives a service van stocked with specific replacement parts. Their current real-time positions are captured in GPS tracking CSV files that reside among your local files; each record in those files identifies the technician, their coordinates, and the zone they currently occupy. No other source carries this positioning data, so the tracking files must be consulted to determine where each technician is starting from.

Pending jobs each carry a priority tier, a minimum required skill level, an estimated duration in minutes, and a customer-requested arrival window. The dispatch system can be queried for paginated lists of technicians or jobs, for a single technician's or job's full details, for the parts inventory of a specific van, for the parts a specific job requires, and for traffic information between two zones. Each job also has a physical site with access constraints documented in JSON site-detail files stored alongside the tracking data; those records specify the hours during which a technician may actually enter the premises. The effective arrival window for any job is the intersection of the customer's requested window and the site's access hours, and a technician's estimated arrival must fall within that effective window.

Travel time between two zones is determined by querying the dispatch system for the traffic segment connecting them, which returns a base travel time in minutes, a congestion factor, and a road-condition rating. To produce the actual travel time, multiply the base time by the congestion factor, then by the road-condition multiplier defined in the JSON scheduling-rules configuration file found among your local files, and finally by any destination-zone adjustment factor listed in the CSV travel-adjustments file (use a factor of one when the destination zone has no entry in that file). For travel within the same zone, use the fixed intra-zone travel time stated in the scheduling-rules file.

Certain replacement parts may serve as acceptable substitutes for others; the allowed pairings are specified in a JSON substitution-rules file among your local files. A job's parts requirement is met when, for every part it demands, the assigned van carries that part or an allowed substitute in sufficient quantity. Parts consumed by one job on a route are no longer available for subsequent jobs on that same technician's route.

A YAML priority-configuration file defines the tier ordering rule: jobs with a numerically lower tier must be assigned ahead of those with a higher tier, and among jobs sharing the same tier, those whose customer window closes earliest take precedence. Within each technician's route the stop sequence must mirror this same ordering: higher-priority stops come first, and ties within a tier are broken by earliest deadline. The scheduling-rules file also caps the number of jobs any single technician may handle in one shift.

When selecting a technician for a job, choose the eligible technician whose current or last-completed-stop zone yields the shortest travel time to the job's zone. If two technicians have identical shortest travel time, break the tie by choosing the one with the lower technician identifier. A technician is eligible only if their skill level meets or exceeds the job's requirement, their van can satisfy the parts requirement, they have not already reached the per-shift job cap, and they can arrive within the effective window and complete the job before their shift ends.
