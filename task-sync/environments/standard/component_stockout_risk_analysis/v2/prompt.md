The manufacturing operation you are supporting relies on a complex supply chain where finished products are assembled from sub-assemblies and raw materials according to multi-level bills of materials. Active production orders drive demand for these components, and any shortfall between the quantities required and what is currently available in stock creates a stockout risk that could halt production lines.

You need to analyze every currently active production order and trace its component requirements through the entire bill of materials hierarchy. Each production order specifies a product and a quantity to be produced. The bill of materials defines how each product breaks down into direct children, which may be sub-assemblies or leaf-level parts. Sub-assemblies further decompose into their own child components. For each component at the leaf level of this hierarchy, compute the total demand driven by all active orders, accounting for the multiplication of quantities through each level of the BOM. Sub-assemblies themselves are also consumed and should be included in the demand analysis. To arrive at accurate demand figures, you must also apply demand adjustment factors documented in the demand forecast files found in your working directory, multiplying each order's derived component demand by the adjustment factor associated with that order's product.

Once the total adjusted demand per component has been established, determine the available inventory for each component by summing on-hand quantities across all warehouses and then subtracting any reserved quantities that are documented in the warehouse status reports in your working directory. Whenever the resulting available inventory is negative, treat it as zero. A component is considered at risk of stockout when its total adjusted demand exceeds its available inventory, and the difference represents the shortage quantity.

For every component flagged as at risk, you must identify the appropriate supplier to include in the alert. The system tracks which suppliers are designated as preferred for each component. When a preferred supplier exists, use that supplier's information. When no preferred supplier is available, choose the supplier that offers the shortest effective lead time for that component. The effective lead time is the base supply lead time from the system plus any additional lead time adjustment days recorded in the supplier performance reports in your working directory. If a particular supplier does not appear in any of those performance reports, assume an adjustment of zero days.

You are required to generate a prioritized procurement alert list covering all at-risk components. The exact methodology for computing priority scores and assigning priority tiers is fully specified in the configuration and rules files within your working directory. Read those files carefully and follow their instructions precisely, as they define the scoring formula, the weights, the thresholds, and every detail needed to produce correct results.

Save this alert list as `procurement_alerts.csv` in your working directory, with columns in this exact order: `component_id`, `component_name`, `total_demand`, `available_inventory`, `shortage_quantity`, `preferred_supplier_id`, `effective_lead_time_days`, `priority_score`, `priority_tier`. Format `total_demand`, `available_inventory`, `shortage_quantity`, and `priority_score` to exactly two decimal places. The `effective_lead_time_days` column should contain integer values. Sort the rows by `priority_score` descending, breaking ties by `component_id` ascending.

In your final response, state the total count of components identified as at risk, name the top three components by priority score together with their shortage quantities and priority tiers, and provide a breakdown of how many components fall into each priority tier.
